===== 5. HAMEL HUSAIN STYLE =====

𝗕𝗲𝗳𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝗬𝗼𝘂 𝗟𝗲𝘁 𝗔𝗻 𝗔𝗴𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗠𝗮𝗸𝗲 𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗗𝗲𝗺𝗼 𝗩𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗼𝘀, 𝗧𝗲𝘀𝘁 𝗜𝘁

OpenScreen is a free MIT screen recorder for polished demos. The original repo was archived on 17 October 2026, and the community continuation (getopenscreen/openscreen) now adds an agent skill, an MCP server and a CLI.

The claim in the README is that one sentence to Claude Code, Codex or Cursor gets you a demo video on your pull request. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗥𝗘𝗔𝗗𝗠𝗘 𝗴𝗶𝘃𝗲𝘀 𝗻𝗼 𝗻𝘂𝗺𝗯𝗲𝗿𝘀 𝗼𝗻 𝗵𝗼𝘄 𝗼𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗸𝘀.

What it gives you:

• Auto zoom that follows the cursor, cursor smoothing, backgrounds, motion blur
• Captions made on-device, working offline
• A transcript you can edit, where deleting a sentence cuts it from the video
• MP4 and GIF export with GPU encoding
• A CLI with JSON output, so runs can be scripted and logged

What it doesn't give you:

• Any published measure of agent-made video quality
• A stability promise. The README says to 𝘦𝘹𝘱𝘦𝘤𝘵 𝘳𝘰𝘶𝘨𝘩 𝘦𝘥𝘨𝘦𝘴 𝘢𝘯𝘥 𝘰𝘤𝘤𝘢𝘴𝘪𝘰𝘯𝘢𝘭 𝘣𝘳𝘦𝘢𝘬𝘪𝘯𝘨 𝘤𝘩𝘢𝘯𝘨𝘦𝘴
• A signed Windows build, or good recording on Windows ARM64
• A statement of what AI editing sends to your model provider (it's bring-your-own-key and off by default)

If you want to know whether it's good enough for your team, a small test tells you more than any star count:

• 𝗣𝗶𝗰𝗸 𝗰𝗮𝘀𝗲𝘀: five real features you shipped last month

===== 6. ETHAN MOLLICK STYLE =====

𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗛𝗮𝗽𝗽𝗲𝗻𝘀 𝗧𝗼 𝗔 𝗙𝗿𝗲𝗲 𝗔𝗽𝗽 𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝗜𝘁𝘀 𝗖𝗿𝗲𝗮𝘁𝗼𝗿 𝗪𝗮𝗹𝗸𝘀 𝗔𝘄𝗮𝘆?

Most of us assume software dies when the person behind it stops. OpenScreen is a small case that went the other way.

It's a free app that records your screen and makes the result look professionally edited. Think of a camera operator who zooms in wherever your mouse goes, then tidies the background. Paid tools do this too. Screen Studio is listed at $29 a month.

One person, Siddharth Vaddem, built OpenScreen as a side project. By his own account it reached 𝘢𝘭𝘮𝘰𝘴𝘵 40𝘬 𝘴𝘵𝘢𝘳𝘴 on GitHub and 120𝘬+ 𝘥𝘰𝘸𝘯𝘭𝘰𝘢𝘥𝘴. In June he said he'd achieved what he wanted, and on 𝟭𝟳 𝗢𝗰𝘁𝗼𝗯𝗲𝗿 𝟮𝟬𝟮𝟲 he closed the project.

It didn't end there. Because the code was under the MIT license, which lets anyone reuse it, a group of volunteers continued it in a new home with his approval. They've released several updates since, the latest stable one on 4 November.

They also added something new. 𝗬𝗼𝘂 𝗰𝗮𝗻 𝗻𝗼𝘄 𝗮𝘀𝗸 𝗮𝗻 𝗔𝗜 𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗶𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗺𝗮𝗸𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗱𝗲𝗺𝗼 𝘃𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗼 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝘆𝗼𝘂. It plans the shots, records, edits and hands back a file. And if you fumble a sentence, you delete it from the written transcript and it disappears from the video.

The evidence cuts both ways. The app is free with no paid tier, and captions are made on your own computer. But the maintainers themselves warn of 𝘳𝘰𝘶𝘨𝘩 𝘦𝘥𝘨𝘦𝘴 𝘢𝘯𝘥 𝘰𝘤𝘤𝘢𝘴𝘪𝘰𝘯𝘢𝘭 𝘣𝘳𝘦𝘢𝘬𝘪𝘯𝘨 𝘤𝘩𝘢𝘯𝘨𝘦𝘴, the new project has a much smaller following (about 7.3k stars), and nobody has published how good the AI-made videos are.

So we know the software survived. We don't yet know whether a volunteer group can keep it healthy for years.
