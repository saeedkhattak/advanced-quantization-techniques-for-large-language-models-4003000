===== STORY =====

𝗠𝗼𝘀𝘁 𝗔𝗴𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗦𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝗣𝗮𝗰𝗸𝘀 𝗦𝘁𝗼𝗽 𝗔𝘁 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗣𝘂𝗹𝗹 𝗥𝗲𝗾𝘂𝗲𝘀𝘁. 𝗧𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝗢𝗻𝗲 𝗗𝗼𝗲𝘀𝗻'𝘁.

You're a solo developer. Claude Code or Codex writes the feature in an afternoon. The tests pass and the PR is merged.

Then come the harder questions. Who is this for? What do they use today instead? Does the payment actually work in production? Do you have a privacy policy?

𝗖𝗼𝗱𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗶𝘀 𝗻𝗼𝘄 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗳𝗮𝘀𝘁 𝗽𝗮𝗿𝘁. 𝗘𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘆𝘁𝗵𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮𝗿𝗼𝘂𝗻𝗱 𝗶𝘁 𝗶𝘀 𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝗼𝗻 𝘆𝗼𝘂.

Paweł Huryn's new open-source repo, product-engineering, packs that whole path into 45 agent skills. It's MIT-licensed and at 𝟲𝟯𝟳 𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗿𝘀 today. The README lays the work out in eight steps: start, proposal, plan, build, click-through, release review, release, real users. Then you go round again.

Building is one of the eight.

𝗕𝗲𝗳𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲: the product pack researches the alternatives people use, sizes market segments and picks a beachhead. You get one proposal with a source for every claim, a high-fidelity screen for each key job on sample data, and the open decisions, each with a recommendation. You decide. The build reads what you accepted.

𝗔𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲: the app-stack pack covers sign-in, authorization, billing, transactional email, legal pages, product analytics and uptime monitoring. Legal pages are drafted from the code for you to approve before the first stranger is asked for anything.

The README's line for it: "𝘕𝘰𝘣𝘰𝘥𝘺 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘴 𝘶𝘯𝘵𝘪𝘭 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘮𝘦𝘯𝘵 𝘸𝘰𝘳𝘬𝘴 𝘪𝘯 𝘱𝘳𝘰𝘥𝘶𝘤𝘵𝘪𝘰𝘯."

The rules come with receipts, 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝟳𝟬𝟬 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗺, dated, from three of his live products. One example from AskOne: the plans were set up by hand in a billing dashboard, a feature key didn't match the code, and 𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘆 𝗽𝗮𝘆𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮𝗰𝗰𝗼𝘂𝗻𝘁 𝘀𝗶𝗹𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗹𝘆 𝗳𝗲𝗹𝗹 𝗯𝗮𝗰𝗸 𝘁𝗼 𝗙𝗿𝗲𝗲. That's now a rule.

Code review is in there too (a fresh agent checks every deploying push), but that part isn't new. gstack and Superpowers both do review. Superpowers stops at the branch. gstack goes as far as deploy and a health check. This one keeps going to billing and legal pages.

Some caveats. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗮𝗽𝗽-𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗰𝗸 𝗮𝘀𝘀𝘂𝗺𝗲𝘀 𝗡𝗲𝘅𝘁.𝗷𝘀, 𝗦𝘂𝗽𝗮𝗯𝗮𝘀𝗲, 𝗖𝗹𝗲𝗿𝗸 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗡𝗲𝘁𝗹𝗶𝗳𝘆. By the README's own count, 28 skills come from real products, 14 from established methods, and a few rest on thinner evidence. The 41 auto-loading skills cost 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝟮,𝟳𝟬𝟬 𝘁𝗼𝗸𝗲𝗻𝘀 𝗮 𝘁𝘂𝗿𝗻 in Claude Code.

𝗙𝗼𝗿 𝗮 𝘀𝗼𝗹𝗼 𝗱𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗹𝗼𝗽𝗲𝗿, 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗷𝗼𝗯 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝘂𝗿𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗼 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝘁 𝗲𝗻𝗴𝗶𝗻𝗲𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗻𝗴. 𝗪𝗿𝗶𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲 𝗶𝘀 𝗼𝗻𝗲 𝘀𝘁𝗲𝗽 𝗼𝗳 𝗲𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁.

===== KARPATHY STYLE =====

𝗖𝗼𝗱𝗲 𝗜𝘀 𝗢𝗻𝗲 𝗦𝘁𝗲𝗽 𝗢𝘂𝘁 𝗢𝗳 𝗘𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁

Paweł Huryn put out a repo called product-engineering: 45 agent skills, MIT, for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Gemini CLI and others.

𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗽𝗮𝗿𝘁 𝗶𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗮𝗻𝘆 𝘀𝗶𝗻𝗴𝗹𝗲 𝘀𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹. 𝗜𝘁'𝘀 𝘄𝗵𝗲𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗹𝗹𝗲𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝗽𝘂𝘁𝘀 𝗶𝘁𝘀 𝗯𝗼𝘂𝗻𝗱𝗮𝗿𝗶𝗲𝘀.

Most coding-agent setups are drawn around the code. Start from a prompt, end at a merged PR. That made sense when writing code was the slow, expensive step.

When an agent writes a feature in an hour, the bottleneck moves. It sits at the two ends: deciding what to build, and getting it to someone who pays. A bit like a factory that suddenly gets a very fast machine in the middle. The line doesn't speed up until you fix the steps before and after.

This repo is drawn around those ends. The README splits the work into eight steps, and build is step four. Before it: research on the alternatives and the segment, then a proposal where every claim has a source and every open decision has a recommendation. After it: click-through, release review, release, real users. The app-stack pack handles auth, billing, email, legal pages, analytics and monitoring.

The README puts the end condition plainly: "𝘕𝘰𝘣𝘰𝘥𝘺 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘴 𝘶𝘯𝘵𝘪𝘭 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘮𝘦𝘯𝘵 𝘸𝘰𝘳𝘬𝘴 𝘪𝘯 𝘱𝘳𝘰𝘥𝘶𝘤𝘵𝘪𝘰𝘯."

I think that's the right way to cut the problem for one person working with agents. The human's job moves to the parts that need taste and accountability: picking the segment, accepting the proposal, clicking through, saying go.

What to hold loosely: it's one builder's method, learned on three products (about 700 dated rules), and the stack is fixed (Next.js, Supabase, Clerk, Netlify). There's no outcome data yet beyond those products.

𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝗖𝗼𝗱𝗲 𝗜𝘀 𝗖𝗵𝗲𝗮𝗽, 𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗜𝘀 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗗𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗹𝗼𝗽𝗲𝗿'𝘀 𝗝𝗼𝗯?

AI coding agents can now write a working feature in an afternoon. That changes the question for anyone building alone. If the code isn't the hard part, what is?

Paweł Huryn, who writes The Product Compass newsletter, has released one answer as a free toolkit called product-engineering. It's 𝟰𝟱 "𝘀𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹𝘀", written instructions that agents like Claude Code and Codex load when a task matches.

𝗜𝘁𝘀 𝗮𝗻𝘀𝘄𝗲𝗿 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗵𝗮𝗿𝗱 𝗽𝗮𝗿𝘁𝘀 𝘀𝗶𝘁 𝗯𝗲𝗳𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗮𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲. Before: who is this for, what do they use today, and why would they switch. After: sign-in, payments, privacy pages, emails, and knowing when the site is down.

Think of a restaurant. A great cook matters, but someone still has to choose the menu, take payments and pass the health inspection. The toolkit splits the work into eight steps, and cooking (writing the code) is just one of them. The README sums up its finish line: "𝘕𝘰𝘣𝘰𝘥𝘺 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘴 𝘶𝘯𝘵𝘪𝘭 𝘱𝘢𝘺𝘮𝘦𝘯𝘵 𝘸𝘰𝘳𝘬𝘴 𝘪𝘯 𝘱𝘳𝘰𝘥𝘶𝘤𝘵𝘪𝘰𝘯."

The person stays in charge of the decisions. Agents research the market and draft a proposal with sources. You accept it or change it, click through the result, and say go.

There's evidence on both sides. For: the author built three live products this way and kept about 700 dated notes on what went wrong, including one where every paying customer was quietly put back on the free plan.

Against: it's one person's method on one set of tools, with no data yet from other builders. The README itself says some parts rest on thin evidence.

What seems clear is that for solo builders, more of the job is now product work.
