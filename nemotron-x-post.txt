Anyone who has read an AI meeting summary knows this mistake. The words are right, but they're pinned on the wrong person.

That job has a name, speaker diarization. It labels each word as Speaker 1, Speaker 2 and so on. The usual accuracy score, word error rate, doesn't measure it at all.

Artificial Analysis has now started ranking speech-to-text services on it. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝘁𝗼𝗽 𝘀𝗽𝗼𝘁 𝘄𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗮𝗻 𝗼𝗽𝗲𝗻-𝘄𝗲𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁 𝗺𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹, 𝗡𝘃𝗶𝗱𝗶𝗮'𝘀 𝗡𝗲𝗺𝗼𝘁𝗿𝗼𝗻 𝟯.𝟱 𝗧𝗿𝗮𝗻𝘀𝗰𝗿𝗶𝗯𝗲.

The score is called WDER, word diarization error rate. Take every word in the correct transcript. Count the ones given to the wrong speaker, plus the ones missed. Divide by the total.

===== 2. KARPATHY STYLE =====

𝗪𝗼𝗿𝗱 𝗘𝗿𝗿𝗼𝗿 𝗥𝗮𝘁𝗲 𝗪𝗮𝘀 𝗛𝗶𝗱𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗔 𝗪𝗵𝗼𝗹𝗲 𝗙𝗮𝗶𝗹𝘂𝗿𝗲 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲

Artificial Analysis added a diarization index to its speech-to-text leaderboard. Nvidia's Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is on top at 𝟯.𝟯%, with Mistral's Voxtral Small Transcribe 3 at 7.5% and Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-2 at 8.8%.

The ranking is fine, but I think the metric is the more interesting part.

The usual diarization score (DER) measures seconds of audio given to the wrong speaker. That makes it sensitive to exactly where a word's timestamp starts and ends. WDER asks something simpler: 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝗲𝗮𝗰𝗵 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗱, 𝘄𝗮𝘀 𝗶𝘁 𝗮𝘁𝘁𝗮𝗰𝗵𝗲𝗱 𝘁𝗼 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗿𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁 𝗽𝗲𝗿𝘀𝗼𝗻? Wrong-speaker words plus missed words, divided by all the words.

It's like grading a court stenographer on whether each line sits under the right name, and ignoring how precisely the clock was stamped.

What I find telling is how the error grows. Nemotron is at 𝟭.𝟳% with two speakers and 𝟱.𝟲% with five to eight. Everyone else in the index goes past 10% in that last band. Two-person calls look close to solved. Rooms don't.

On the architecture, Nvidia put a speaker encoder (from its earlier Nemotron 3 Diarization model) next to the speech encoder and fed both into one language model, 34B parameters with about 4.3B active. So the model that writes the words also sees who's speaking. Imo that's a plausible reason it does well here, though this benchmark can't prove it.

Now the caveats. 𝟵𝟳 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘀𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀, 𝗘𝗻𝗴𝗹𝗶𝘀𝗵 𝗼𝗻𝗹𝘆, 𝗺𝗼𝘀𝘁𝗹𝘆 𝗽𝗿𝗶𝘃𝗮𝘁𝗲 𝗮𝘂𝗱𝗶𝗼. Nemotron was tested through a single DeepInfra endpoint. And it runs at 63.5x real time against MAI-Transcribe-2's 313.9x.

𝘞𝘩𝘢𝘵 𝘐'𝘥 𝘭𝘪𝘬𝘦 𝘵𝘰 𝘴𝘦𝘦 𝘯𝘦𝘹𝘵 𝘪𝘴 𝘵𝘩𝘦 𝘴𝘢𝘮𝘦 𝘵𝘢𝘣𝘭𝘦 𝘧𝘰𝘳 𝘯𝘰𝘯-𝘌𝘯𝘨𝘭𝘪𝘴𝘩 𝘢𝘶𝘥𝘪𝘰, 𝘢𝘯𝘥 𝘧𝘰𝘳 𝘰𝘷𝘦𝘳𝘭𝘢𝘱𝘱𝘪𝘯𝘨 𝘴𝘱𝘦𝘦𝘤𝘩 𝘳𝘦𝘱𝘰𝘳𝘵𝘦𝘥 𝘰𝘯 𝘪𝘵𝘴 𝘰𝘸𝘯.

===== 6. ETHAN MOLLICK STYLE =====

𝗖𝗮𝗻 𝗔𝗜 𝗙𝗶𝗻𝗮𝗹𝗹𝘆 𝗧𝗲𝗹𝗹 𝗪𝗵𝗼'𝘀 𝗧𝗮𝗹𝗸𝗶𝗻𝗴?

AI has become very good at writing down what was said. It's been much weaker at a question people find easy: who said it?

A new ranking from Artificial Analysis measures exactly that. It played 97 recorded English conversations, with two to eight people in each, to a set of transcription services. Then it checked every word to see if it was credited to the right speaker.
