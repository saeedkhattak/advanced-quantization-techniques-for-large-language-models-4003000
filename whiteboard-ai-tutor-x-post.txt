===== 1. STORY =====

A student asks why we have seasons. The AI tutor writes the correct answer on a whiteboard, draws two Earths around the Sun, and labels the one leaning toward the Sun "𝘕𝘰𝘳𝘵𝘩: 𝘸𝘪𝘯𝘵𝘦𝘳".

The model never touches a canvas. It streams small JSON steps, each with one line of narration and a few drawing operations. A player in the browser turns them into handwriting.

===== 2. KARPATHY STYLE =====

𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗧𝘂𝘁𝗼𝗿 𝗜𝘀 𝗮 𝗣𝗿𝗼𝘁𝗼𝗰𝗼𝗹, 𝗡𝗼𝘁 𝗮 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹

There's a nice small repo called whiteboard-ai-tutor. An LLM teaches on a whiteboard: it writes, draws, erases, and the student can cut in.

It looks like a new model capability. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗽𝗮𝗿𝘁 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗶𝘁'𝘀 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝟲 𝗱𝗿𝗮𝘄𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗼𝗽𝗲𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗮 𝗝𝗦𝗢𝗡 𝘀𝘁𝗿𝗲𝗮𝗺. The model emits steps (a line of narration plus ops like write, line, ellipse, erase) and a browser player draws them as handwriting. No JSON mode, no tool calling, just text.

Think of someone describing a diagram over the phone while a friend draws it. They never see the paper.

And that's exactly where it breaks. The author ran one prompt on six models, 288 calls, and had 64 boards reviewed blind. 𝟰𝟰 𝗽𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗲𝗱. Of the 20 failures, 𝟭𝟮 𝘄𝗲𝗿𝗲 𝗳𝗶𝗴𝘂𝗿𝗲𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘁𝗿𝗮𝗱𝗶𝗰𝘁𝗲𝗱 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘁𝗲𝘅𝘁: the season label on the wrong Earth, the equilibrium point 35 to 80 px away from where the lines actually cross. The written statements were almost never wrong.

So the models know the lesson and can't do the coordinate arithmetic reliably. I think that's the expected result when a language model has to pick x and y by hand.

More thinking didn't fix it. On three of the six models the first stroke then took 𝟯𝟬 𝘁𝗼 𝟭𝟮𝟬 𝘀, and 23 calls hit the 8,000-token cap before the board was finished.

Caveats: 11 boards per model, one prompt (written in Chinese), an LLM reviewer, and 𝗻𝗼 𝗵𝘂𝗺𝗮𝗻 𝗮𝘂𝗱𝗶𝘁 𝘆𝗲𝘁. Gemini 4 Argon at 11 of 11 against DeepSeek with thinking off at 4 or 5 of 10 is a real gap. The middle of the table isn't.

What I'd want to see next is what the README already proposes: ops like 𝚖𝚊𝚛𝚔_𝚒𝚗𝚝𝚎𝚛𝚜𝚎𝚌𝚝𝚒𝚘𝚗(𝚊, 𝚋) where code computes the point, and a second pass where the model looks at a screenshot of its own board.

===== 5. HAMEL HUSAIN STYLE =====

𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗟𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗜𝘀 𝗡𝗼𝘁 𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗘𝘃𝗮𝗹

whiteboard-ai-tutor is a small benchmark of six LLMs teaching on a whiteboard. The most useful table in it has nothing to do with which model won.

The author wrote an automatic linter for the boards: off-board objects, overlapping text, strokes crossing labels. Then 64 boards went through a blind review against a fixed rubric.

The linter flagged 𝟰𝟭 𝗼𝗳 𝟲𝟰 𝗯𝗼𝗮𝗿𝗱𝘀 for hard errors. The blind review 𝗽𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗲𝗱 𝟮𝟲 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗼𝘀𝗲. And 5 boards the linter called clean were wrong.

It punished rich diagrams, where labels sit near lines. It couldn't see the main failure at all, a figure that disagrees with its own text. That was 𝟭𝟮 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝟮𝟬 𝗳𝗮𝗶𝗹𝗲𝗱 𝗯𝗼𝗮𝗿𝗱𝘀. Gemini 4 Argon scored 21 to 29% linter-clean and passed 11 of 11 in review.

𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗿𝗲𝗽𝗼 𝗴𝗶𝘃𝗲𝘀 𝘆𝗼𝘂: every raw output from 288 calls, 64 rendered boards with per-board notes, a rubric, and a blind setup where scores were written to disk before model names were revealed.

𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗶𝘁 𝗱𝗼𝗲𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗴𝗶𝘃𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂: a human-verified judge. The reviewer was an LLM, and the 18-board human audit sheet exists but 𝗵𝗮𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗯𝗲𝗲𝗻 𝗳𝗶𝗹𝗹𝗲𝗱 𝗶𝗻. There are also only 11 boards per model.

• 𝗟𝗼𝗼𝗸 𝗳𝗶𝗿𝘀𝘁: open the boards and read them yourself. The gallery is public
• 𝗡𝗮𝗺𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗳𝗮𝗶𝗹𝘂𝗿𝗲: here it's "figure contradicts text", not "bad layout"

===== 6. ETHAN MOLLICK STYLE =====

𝗖𝗮𝗻 𝗮𝗻 𝗔𝗜 𝗧𝗲𝗮𝗰𝗵 𝗮𝘁 𝗮 𝗪𝗵𝗶𝘁𝗲𝗯𝗼𝗮𝗿𝗱?

A good teacher doesn't only talk. They write, sketch, rub something out, and stop when a hand goes up. So how close is AI to doing that?

A small open-source experiment gives a partial answer. Six AI models got the same instructions and the same questions (why we have seasons, why the Pythagorean theorem works, what happens to price when demand rises). Each had to teach on a whiteboard.

The models can't see the board. They work like someone giving drawing directions over the phone: "𝘸𝘳𝘪𝘵𝘦 𝘵𝘩𝘪𝘴 𝘩𝘦𝘳𝘦, 𝘥𝘳𝘢𝘸 𝘢 𝘤𝘪𝘳𝘤𝘭𝘦 𝘵𝘩𝘦𝘳𝘦". A program does the actual drawing.

𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗴𝗼𝗼𝗱 𝗻𝗲𝘄𝘀: all six could do it, and of 64 boards checked blind, 𝟰𝟰 𝗽𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗲𝗱. When a student interrupted, 𝗮𝗹𝗹 𝟮𝟰 𝗯𝗼𝗮𝗿𝗱𝘀 𝗮𝗻𝘀𝘄𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗱 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗾𝘂𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻.
